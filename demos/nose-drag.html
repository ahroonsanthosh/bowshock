Nose wave drag and total drag

The inviscid pressure (wave) drag of a rocket's nose and body ahead of the base, in supersonic flow at zero incidence: not the rocket's total drag, which also has skin friction, base drag and fins. Each case is solved on successively finer grids, and its error band is measured from how the drag changes between them. The total drag, below, adds the other components to that solve. What this computes, how it is checked, and what is refused

What this is: the inviscid pressure (wave) drag coefficient of the nose and the body ahead of the base, on the body's cross-sectional area, at zero incidence. It excludes skin friction, base drag and fins, so it is not a rocket's total drag. The bow shock is fitted: it is the outer boundary of the grid, with the exact jump conditions across it, and the error falls at close to second order in the cell size (observed orders 1.6 to 2.4). The value shown is the finest grid's; its band is measured from the grids and includes an allowance of 7e-5 of the value for an error the grid family cannot show. Checked against exact or converged reference solutions (design record 0006 in the repository) on three grids for cones of 5 to 30 deg at Mach 1.3 to 3 (Taylor-Maccoll) and for ogives, secant ogives and parabolic noses of 3 to 5 calibers at Mach 1.5 to 4 (a method of characteristics), and on two grids for cones at Mach 3 and 5: every band measured contains the error of the value shown, and where the order has settled the value extrapolated from three grids lies within 3.4e-5 of the reference. On 10-caliber noses at Mach 1.2 and 1.5, which converge faster than second order, that extrapolation misses by up to 6.0e-4, but their bands still contain their errors (by 1.56 to 2.05 times). Other shapes, lengths and Mach numbers are computed the same way but have not been compared with a reference. Ellipsoid noses, whose tip is blunt, stand the shock off ahead of the tip, on a grid wrapped round the nose: behind the sonic line their drag is checked against a method of characteristics started from the solution (within 1e-5 of the nose's drag on three noses at Mach 2 and 3); ahead of it, where the flow is subsonic and no independent reference exists, by the grids' convergence and the exact stagnation pressure. At Mach 1.2 to 1.5, where the shock stands far off, a short body behind an ellipsoid is solved longer than asked, to keep the grid's cells even (the drag is still that of the body asked for, whose flow leaves its end supersonically); four such noses converge on three grids at observed orders 1.91 to 2.17, and behind the sonic line the method of characteristics agrees within 3.2e-6 of the nose's drag at Mach 1.5 and within 6.3e-5 at Mach 1.2, where the flow it starts from is barely supersonic. Other blunt noses (Von Karman and the other Haack noses, the power series) are not served yet. Refused, with the reason: tips whose shock detaches; tips whose flow crosses the solver's starting plane, just behind the tip, at an axial Mach number below 1, except where the flow on the tip's surface is supersonic and the nose meets the body without a corner (measured on a 3-caliber tangent ogive at Mach 1.35: two differently laid out grid families agree within 1.5e-5); of 40 noses of 1 to 3 calibers tried at Mach 1.2 before that exception, 19 detach their shock and 13 were refused by this test; tips steeper than about 40 deg (a limit of the present grids, set on measurement); bodies whose flow at their end is subsonic; sharp noses whose grids fail the quality checks of fitted grids even with 1.5 times the columns along the body (a family solved again that way where the usual one fails is served, with the reason); the 6-caliber ellipsoid at Mach 1.2, whose wall cells near the tip are too skewed for the present grids (of ellipsoids of 0.5 to 6 calibers with 1, 2 or 5 calibers of body at Mach 1.2, 1.3 and 1.5, the only ones refused on the coarsest grid).

Total drag

What the total includes, and how far it is validated

The rocket's total zero-incidence, power-off drag coefficient on the body's cross-section: the nose wave drag solved above, with its band, plus the body's skin friction, whose band is validated on wind-tunnel and flight data, and the fins' friction, wave drag and edges and the base, whose bands are estimated on data of each component and tested only in the total (design records 0007 to 0010; each component's label says what it is). The bands add linearly. One class of rocket has passed a test of the total against free-flight data, the Basic Finner's, and its tolerance was wide: -19.5% to -26.2% and +23.1% to +27.4% of the modelled total, so a pass says little by itself. The class runs from Mach 1.8 to 2.15 only: above it the fins-on-base factor was read from one of Love's estimates in TN 3819 fig. 35, not from its free-flight curve. Inside that class the total is labelled validated; outside it, an estimate, not validated. Where a component's method does not cover the case, the total is refused, with every reason, and no number is given. A flat plate's rounded edge is a full radius, half its thickness; a rounded edge is served where the Mach number normal to it, M cos(sweep), is 1.0 or more; square-cut leading edges and rounded trailing edges are refused (design record 0016). The defaults are the Basic Finner's shape, 30 mm across, at sea level; its button sets the range's conditions.

The flight condition, the standard atmosphere, and the Basic Finner's inputs

The altitude is above mean sea level, in the U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (NOAA-S/T 76-1562, NASA-TM-X-74335), served from 5,000 m below sea level to 51,412 m, the top of the stratopause, and tested against the document's own tables (design record 0011, section 9). It gives the air's temperature, speed of sound and viscosity. The speed is the Mach number times the speed of sound; the Reynolds number is that speed times the overall length (its calibers times the diameter) over the kinematic viscosity. Real air departs from the standard one (a hot day is warmer): when you know the Reynolds number and temperature, give them directly.

The Basic Finner's range air was 293.95 K, as warm as the standard atmosphere only about 890 m below sea level, so its button gives the range's Reynolds number (about 6.6e6 times the Mach number) and temperature directly. The gate's shots flew in that air; the validated class takes static temperatures of 216.65 to 320 K, over which the modelled total moves by at most 0.13% (design record 0011, section 3), and its Reynolds numbers, 1.19e7 to 2.0e7. The gate's inputs (placeholders above): a laminar run to Re_T 5e5, 1.6e6 and 3e6 from the tip and from each fin's leading edge, and the wall at the air temperature. No roughness model is validated: a roughness band is yours, shown with the source you give.
